Power resilience at home: a practical tech plan

Keep the plan simple: decide what matters, estimate the energy needed, and test the equipment before relying on it.

Start with a normal day

Resilience means having a workable second option when your usual setup is unavailable. For household technology, that might mean a charged phone, a spare cable, a copy of important information and enough battery capacity for the devices you actually need.

Write down what would be inconvenient to lose and what would be genuinely important. A laptop deadline and a garden speaker are different priorities. Decide what can wait before buying equipment.

Make a device list

For each item, record how you charge it, its power draw if known, and how long you want to use it. Check the device label or manual rather than treating the charger’s maximum rating as a measurement of normal use.

DeviceQuestion to answerFirst step
PhoneWill my cable work with the battery I own?Try a complete charge before relying on it.
LaptopDoes it accept charging from that port and supply?Check the laptop’s charging requirements.
Router and digital phoneWhat must stay powered for calls to work?Ask the provider about your particular setup.
LightingWhere will I need light?Keep a charged torch somewhere easy to find.

Power bank, power station or UPS?

A phone-sized power bank is convenient for portable USB devices. A power station offers more capacity and, on some models, mains-style sockets. A UPS is designed for a particular continuity role. These are different jobs: check the manufacturer’s specifications rather than assuming any battery will switch over automatically or power every appliance.

Capacity and output are different

Capacity tells you how much energy can be stored. Output tells you how much power can be supplied at once. A battery can have enough stored energy but still be unsuitable for a device that needs more output than it can deliver.

For an initial estimate, divide usable watt-hours by the device’s average watts. Conversion losses and changing demand reduce the certainty of that figure. Treat it as a planning estimate and test your real setup.

Example: a working evening

Suppose, purely as a calculation example, you have 500Wh of usable energy and a steady 50W load. The arithmetic gives ten hours. That is not a promise about any product: real devices change their demand and a battery’s usable capacity depends on the setup.

Make the plan smaller if the result is unrealistic. Save documents locally, finish essential tasks first and switch off equipment you do not need.

Connectivity needs its own plan

Ofcom explains that digital landlines need power, and may stop working during a power cut without a suitable alternative. Keeping a router running does not, by itself, establish that calls or the wider network will remain available. Ask your provider what applies to your service, especially if you depend on a landline.

Keep information accessible

Keep important contact numbers somewhere you can reach without signing in to an online account. Decide where household members can find chargers, lights and the equipment instructions. A simple written plan is easier to use than a collection of unopened boxes.

Solar is an addition to the plan

Check panel and power-station compatibility before combining them. Plan around a charged battery first, then think about whether outdoor charging suits your location and the time of year. Do not base an important task on an untested expectation of solar output.

Test, label and maintain

  1. Charge the equipment and check its instructions.
  2. Try each intended device with the correct cable.
  3. Record what worked and what still needs checking.
  4. Label the cables and keep the kit together.
  5. Set your own reminder to check charge levels and repeat the test.

Use equipment within its manufacturer’s limits. A portable battery is not a substitute for a professionally designed household electrical installation. If your plan involves fixed wiring, arrange qualified advice.

Where to start

Our recommendation is to use what you already own first. Find the gaps by testing. Buy additional equipment only when you can explain which device it supports and for how long.
